## Lucio Fontana (1899–1968, Rosario, Argentina / Milan, Italy)

Lucio Fontana was an Argentine-Italian artist whose work radically questioned the boundaries of painting, sculpture, and space. Born in Rosario to Italian parents, he studied in Milan, worked between Argentina and Italy, and developed one of the most consequential theoretical frameworks in postwar art: **Spatialism** (*Spazialismo*).

### Spatialism: Art Beyond the Canvas

Fontana's first *Manifesto Blanco* (White Manifesto, Buenos Aires, 1946) — written with students — called for art to abandon the "illusory space" of the canvas surface and to embrace real space, real time, movement, and technology as new dimensions of creation. Between 1947 and 1952 he published five more Spatialist manifestos in Milan.

The manifesto's core argument:

> *"Matter, colour, and sound in motion are the phenomena whose simultaneous development makes up the new art."*  
> — Manifesto Blanco, 1946

This demand — that art incorporate time, space, and energy rather than simulate them — anticipates, structurally, what Chapter 5 calls the "expanded artwork": a work that exists across multiple states (physical, digital, video, certified) rather than as a fixed object.

### The *Tagli* (Cuts) and the *Buchi* (Holes)

Fontana's most iconic gestures are:

- **Buchi** (holes, 1949–ongoing): punctures in the canvas surface that literally open painting into physical space behind it.
- **Tagli** (cuts, 1958–1968): single or multiple slashes through a monochrome canvas with a sharp blade. The *Concetti Spaziali, Attesa* (Spatial Concepts, Waiting) series consists of canvases cut with a single vertical slash, making the void — the space behind — an element of the work.

Both gestures materialize the same impulse: breaking the illusory plane to make real space visible. The cut does not destroy the canvas; it opens it.

### Chapter 5 and Fontana: Two Echoes

The chapter invokes Fontana at two distinct moments:

**§5.3.1.5 — From Fontana to AI Brushes**  
AI outpainting is read as a contemporary extension of the Spatialist ambition: painting literally escapes the bounded frame and "enters an augmented — albeit virtual — artistic space: one that is potentially infinite, generated by computation, where the painted image grows and transforms." The AI is described as an "algorithmic brush that paints following rules derived from vast archives of images" — an indirect gesture, like a composer writing a score for an interpreter.

**§5.4 — From Manual to Virtual Sculpture**  
Fontana's *Manifesto of Spatialism* (1947) is cited as the conceptual precedent for virtual sculpture's liberation from the closed object: "to embrace real space and real time as new dimensions of art." Digital sculpture operates in gravity-free, infinitely scalable, reversible space — the fullest material realization of what Fontana argued for in theory.

| Fontana's gesture | Chapter 5 counterpart |
|------------------|-----------------------|
| Cut opens canvas into real space | AI outpainting opens image into virtual space |
| Manifesto calls for space+time in art | Virtual sculpture: scalable, reversible, gravity-free |
| Work exists in void, not surface | Expanded artwork: physical + digital + video states |
| Spatialism as art theory | Post-human identity as artistic condition |

### Key Works

- *Concetto Spaziale* (Spatial Concept), 1949 — punctured canvas, various dimensions
- *Concetto Spaziale, Attesa* (Spatial Concept, Waiting), 1960 — single-cut monochrome, Museum of Modern Art, New York / Tate Modern, London
- *Ambiente Spaziale a Luce Nera* (Spatial Environment in Black Light), 1949 — installation with ultraviolet light; first spatial environment in art history
- *New York* series, 1962 — cuts on shaped canvases following the artist's visits to the city
