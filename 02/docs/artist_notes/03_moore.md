# Henry Moore — Maquettes and the Sculptural Sketch

## Henry Moore (1898–1986, Castleford, Yorkshire)

British sculptor. Moore developed a systematic use of small-scale maquettes — hand-modeled in plaster, terracotta, or clay — as projective design tools. His practice of working from natural fragments (stones, bones, shells found on the shore) to large monumental bronzes anticipates the contemporary hybrid workflow.

**Practice relevant to Chapter 2:**
- **Maquette as matrix**: Moore made dozens of small studies before committing to a monumental work. Each maquette was a test of spatial relationships, mass, void, and gesture — equivalent to iterating in a digital fluid matrix.
- **Material collection**: He gathered natural objects as formal seeds — holes in flint, the arch of a rib — from which larger structural ideas grew. The chapter draws a direct parallel: "As Henry Moore often began from small natural fragments — a stone, a shell — these modest starting points suggest how practice can grow from the incidental toward the monumental."
- **Tension between mass and void**: Moore's exploration of the hole as positive form anticipates Boolean operations and the spatial opening of Fontana's cuts.

**Key works:**
- *Reclining Figure* series (1936–1984) — multiple versions in plaster, bronze, marble
- *Nuclear Energy* (1967) — University of Chicago. Moore called it "neither frightening nor reassuring" — a form that must be held in the mind and body simultaneously.
- *Large Two Forms* (1969) — Henry Moore Foundation

**Connection to digital practice:**
The chapter positions virtual modeling as the contemporary equivalent of the maquette: "a drawn maquette in space that extends this exploratory logic into an open, potentially infinite digital continuum." Moore's iterative, non-destructive approach to form finds its computational echo in non-destructive modifiers, version history, and real-time iteration in Blender.
