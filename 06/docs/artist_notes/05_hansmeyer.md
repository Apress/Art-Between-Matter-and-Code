## Michael Hansmeyer (b. 1972, Germany; ETH Zurich)

Michael Hansmeyer is an architect and computational designer working at ETH Zurich whose practice explores the generative possibilities of algorithmic processes applied to architectural form. His work is defined by an extreme position: algorithms not as tools for efficiency optimization, but as engines for generating formal complexity far beyond what human hands or conventional design tools could produce. The physical realization of his computational structures — made possible by additive manufacturing — demonstrates that 3D printing is not simply a faster way to make known shapes, but a technology that enables entirely new categories of form.

### Digital Grotesque I (2013)

*Digital Grotesque I*, created in collaboration with Benjamin Dillenburger, is the most structurally demanding example of algorithmic architecture realized through additive manufacturing. Two 3.2-metre sandstone columns were generated by a recursive subdivision algorithm operating on a simple input form (a cube). At each subdivision step, the algorithm introduces variation in edge curvature, surface modulation, and ornamental detail at progressively finer scales — from the overall column profile down to surface features 1 mm in size.

The resulting geometry contains approximately **260 million individual facets** — a complexity that is geometrically and informationally coherent, yet impossible to design manually. The columns were fabricated using binder jetting (powder-based sandstone printing) at 16 µm resolution, requiring approximately 4 months of printing time. The final objects are solid sandstone, structurally self-supporting, and indistinguishable in material quality from carved stone — yet no human hand shaped any surface.

*Digital Grotesque I* established a benchmark: 3D printing, when combined with sufficient computational complexity in the input geometry, can produce objects whose richness of surface detail exceeds the capacity of traditional craftsmanship.

### Digital Grotesque II (2017) and Grotto (2017)

*Digital Grotesque II* extended the approach from individual columns to a complete immersive architectural space: a full-scale room whose walls, ceiling, and floor are covered in algorithmically generated ornament at the same 16 µm resolution. The visitor enters an environment in which every surface plane has dissolved into recursive detail — a contemporary grotesque in the original sense (from *grottesco*, the decorative vocabulary found in the Domus Aurea's underground rooms).

*Grotto* (2017, Design Miami) is a smaller self-contained version: a cave-like alcove of 3D-printed resin whose interior surface reproduces the same recursive subdivision logic.

### Subdivided Columns (2010–2012)

The *Subdivided Columns* series is the conceptual origin of *Digital Grotesque*: eight columns generated by applying Doo-Sabin and Catmull-Clark subdivision schemes to a simple base mesh. Each column demonstrates a different algorithmic regime, producing forms that range from smooth organic undulation to spiky, crystalline complexity. Exhibited as digital prints and physical models, they established Hansmeyer's reputation for computational form-finding that refuses the smoothed-out aesthetic of most parametric architecture.

### Relevance to Chapter 6

Chapter 6 (§6.2) references Hansmeyer's work as the limit case of geometric complexity enabled by additive manufacturing: forms that are fully resolved computationally but would be impossible to produce by any subtractive or formative fabrication process. The argument is that 3D printing does not merely replicate conventional forms faster — it enables a category of forms that could not previously exist as physical objects. This directly motivates the chapter's discussion of when and why additive manufacturing is the appropriate fabrication choice.
