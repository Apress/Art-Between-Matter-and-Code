## Amy Karle (b. USA; based San Francisco, California)

Amy Karle is an artist, researcher, and futurist working at the intersection of art, biology, technology, and ethics. Her practice uses additive manufacturing, biomaterials, and computational design to explore questions about the body, regeneration, mortality, and the boundaries between natural and artificial life. Karle is among the first artists to use 3D printing not as a tool for producing inert sculptural objects but as a scaffold for biological processes — making her work a defining reference for understanding 3D printing as a platform for living systems.

### Regenerative Reliquary (2016)

*Regenerative Reliquary* is Karle's most significant work and the primary reference in Chapter 6. The piece consists of a 3D-printed scaffold in the form of a human hand — specifically, the form of a hand's internal structure: the bones, tendons, and ligaments, suspended in a bioreactor. The scaffold is fabricated from a hydrogel-based biopolymer (polyethylene glycol diacrylate, PEGDA) using stereolithography (SLA), producing a material that is biocompatible with human cells.

The scaffold was seeded with human mesenchymal stem cells and placed in a culture medium. Over weeks, the stem cells proliferated and differentiated along the structural scaffold, growing living tissue around the digital armature. The work asks a direct question: if a 3D printer can produce a structure that living cells will colonize and transform, what is the status of the "artwork"? Is it the digital file? The printed scaffold? The living tissue that grows within it? The process itself?

The title deliberately invokes the tradition of the reliquary — a vessel that preserves and exhibits sacred bodily remains. *Regenerative Reliquary* is a reliquary for the future body: a scaffold that does not preserve a dead saint's bone but grows a new living tissue.

### Form Following Function (2016) and Inner Garden (2016)

*Form Following Function* (2016) is a series of 3D-printed forms derived from biological data — specifically from the structural analysis of bone, muscle attachment, and joint mechanics. Each form embodies a functional principle: the geometry follows load paths, growth directions, or evolutionary optimization pressures. The works make visible the latent geometry within biological systems.

*Inner Garden* (2016) is a series of wearable 3D-printed forms designed to interface with the surface of the body. Inspired by the idea that the body carries its own interior garden of biological processes, the pieces suggest a future in which clothing and wearables are grown, rather than manufactured, to fit the individual body's specific biological topography.

### Relevance to Chapter 6

Chapter 6 (§6.3) positions Karle's work alongside Neri Oxman's Silk Pavilion as evidence that additive manufacturing can function as a platform for biological creativity — not just as a fabrication tool, but as a generative environment in which living processes complete the work. This expands the virtual-to-physical chain beyond the conventional endpoint (a finished printed object) into a living, evolving system.
