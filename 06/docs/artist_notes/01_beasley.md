## Bruce Beasley (b. 1939, Los Angeles, USA)

Bruce Beasley is one of the earliest American sculptors to integrate computer-aided design directly into a fine-art bronze-casting practice. Working from his studio in Oakland, California, he has produced monumental abstract sculpture for more than six decades, pioneering the use of digital tools at a moment when no consumer software or desktop 3D printing existed — making his work a foundational reference point for any discussion of the virtual-to-physical fabrication chain.

### Early Career and Traditional Casting

Beasley rose to international attention in the 1960s with cast acrylic and polished bronze sculptures that explored transparency, mass, and light. His *Apostrophe* (1963) — acquired by the Los Angeles County Museum of Art when Beasley was just 24 — established him as a significant voice in American abstraction. The work's smooth, reflective volumes anticipate the geometric precision he would later achieve through computational means.

### Computer-Aided Sculpture: The Augustium Series

In the early 1980s, Beasley began collaborating with Evans & Sutherland, a Utah-based computer graphics company that at the time produced workstations used primarily for flight simulation and military visualization. The partnership gave Beasley access to then-cutting-edge 3D modeling software capable of generating complex polyhedral forms that would have been nearly impossible to realize through conventional sketching and maquette-making.

The resulting *Augustium* series (begun c. 1985) translates computer-generated polyhedra into large-scale bronze sculptures. Each piece originated as a wireframe or solid model on the Evans & Sutherland workstation, was output as a physical pattern, and was then cast in bronze using traditional foundry methods. The sculptures are abstract — interlocking, rotating, and interpenetrating geometric forms — with a formal logic that could only have been discovered through computational iteration.

Beasley's process is a direct precedent for the virtual-to-physical chain described in Chapter 6: digital model → form refinement → fabrication pattern → casting. The intermediate steps differed from today's 3D printing workflow (no STL format, no FDM or SLA), but the conceptual structure — using computation to generate forms that are then materialized — is identical.

### Why Beasley in Chapter 6

Chapter 6 (§6.1) cites Beasley as the first artist to successfully and systematically bridge computer modeling and physical casting in a fine-art context. His significance lies not only in technical innovation but in the decision to embrace digital tools not for efficiency, but for formal exploration: the computer allowed him to discover geometries that his hands alone could not have arrived at.
